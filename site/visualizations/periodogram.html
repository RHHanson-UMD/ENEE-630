SPECTRAL ESTIMATION / VERIFIED NOV 13, 2026

Why averaging a periodogram helps.

A longer record does not necessarily make one spectral estimate more reliable. For real Gaussian white noise at zero frequency, a single periodogram remains noisy; averages of independent blocks become more concentrated.

04 / REASONING & ASSUMPTIONS

What the estimates mean

Single periodogram

From N independent samples, the zero-frequency transform is simply their sum.

IN(0) = (1/N) [Σn=0N−1 x[n]]²

The sum is Gaussian with variance Nσ². Its normalized square has the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, no matter how large N gets.

Nonoverlapping block average

Divide the same N = K × L samples into K independent blocks, each length L.

Ī(0) = (1/K) Σm=1K Im(0)

Each block's periodogram has mean σ² and variance 2σ⁴. Independence makes the variance of the average equal to 2σ⁴/K.

Both are unbiased: E[IN(0)] = E[Ī(0)] = σ². Only the average gains consistency as K tends to infinity under this model: Var[Ī(0)] = 2σ⁴/K → 0. Increasing L alone does not reduce the zero-frequency periodogram variance.
How the derivation works

Let S = Σ x[n]. For independent x[n] distributed N(0, σ²), S is N(0, Nσ²). Therefore Z = S/(σ√N) is standard normal, and IN(0) = σ² Z². Since E[Z²] = 1 and Var[Z²] = 2, the expected periodogram is σ² and its variance is 2σ⁴. For K disjoint blocks, their normalized sums are independent standard normals. Averaging K independent squared normals divides the variance by K.

When this conclusion does not directly apply

The exact chi-square-one result here requires real, zero-mean, independent Gaussian samples and frequency zero. For colored noise, non-Gaussian noise, nonzero frequencies, overlapping segments, or other window choices, the variance, bias, and independence structure may change. Segment averaging can also reduce frequency resolution and introduce bias when the underlying spectrum is not flat. These effects require separate analysis.
